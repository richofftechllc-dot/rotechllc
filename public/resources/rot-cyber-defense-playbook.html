The Cyber Defense Playbook

How real companies actually get attacked, how they figure out where they're weak, and how they decide what to spend money on. Written for the person who wants to run security — not just pass a test about it.

First edition · Rich Off Tech LLC · 2026 · Cleared tech. No shortcuts.

Who this is for

You've got the fundamentals — maybe Security+, maybe a help desk seat, maybe a clearance you haven't cashed in yet. You can define confidentiality and integrity. What you can't do yet is walk into a room and answer "where are we exposed and what should we fix first?"

That question is the whole job. It's what separates a $65K analyst from a $130K security engineer, and it's what every hiring manager is actually probing for when they ask you a scenario question. This playbook is that gap, closed.

Nothing here is theory for theory's sake. Every part ends with a drill you can run this week — on your own machine, your own home network, or your current employer's environment if you have permission. Do the drills and you'll have stories to tell in interviews. Skip them and this is just more reading.

1 · Know who's actually coming for you 🎯

"Hackers" is a useless word. It's like saying "criminals" — technically true, tells you nothing about how to lock your door. Defense starts by getting specific about who, because who determines how much they'll spend, how long they'll wait, and what they're after.

TTPs — the vocabulary that makes you sound senior

Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures. Learn the distinction, because interviewers use it as a filter:

  • Tactic = the why. The goal at that stage. "Get initial access." "Escalate privileges." "Move laterally." "Exfiltrate."
  • Technique = the how. "Spear-phishing with a malicious attachment." "Abusing a valid account."
  • Procedure = the exactly how, by this specific crew. "Sends a fake DocuSign link on Tuesday mornings from a lookalike domain registered nine days prior."

MITRE ATT&CK is the free, public catalogue of all of this — a matrix of tactics across the top and techniques underneath. It's the shared language of the industry. When a report says "the actor used T1566.001," everybody knows that means a phishing attachment. Learn to read the matrix and you can read any threat report.

Why it matters for you: Tools and vendors change every two years. Tactics barely change at all. Somebody has to get in, get bigger, look around, and take something — that sequence has been the same for thirty years. Learn the sequence and you're never starting over.

Four attacks worth knowing cold

These are public history. Every security interviewer knows them, and being able to say what actually went wrong — not just the name — reads as real.

  • WannaCry (2017) — ransomware worm that spread through an unpatched Windows file-sharing flaw. A patch existed months before. Lesson: patching is not paperwork, it's the control.
  • NotPetya (2017) — looked like ransomware, was actually destruction. Entered through a compromised update to Ukrainian accounting software, then tore through global companies as collateral. Lesson: your software supply chain is your attack surface, and flat networks turn one infection into all of them.
  • SolarWinds (2020) — attackers compromised the build system of a trusted vendor, so the malicious code was signed and shipped as a legitimate update. Lesson: "trusted" is not a security control. Detection matters when prevention is bypassed by design.
  • Colonial Pipeline (2021) — ransomware via a legacy VPN account with no MFA, still active, password in a leaked dump. The pipeline stopped, half a country queued for gas. Lesson: one forgotten account is a national news story.

Three of those four were preventable with fundamentals: patch, MFA, kill dormant accounts. That's not me being reductive — that's the actual finding in the actual reports.

Drill 1

Pick an actor, map it

Open the MITRE ATT&CK site and pick one named group. Write half a page answering: what industries do they hit, what's their usual way in, what do they do once inside, and which three controls would you put in front of them first — with your reasoning for the order.

Why: "Walk me through a threat actor" is a real interview question. You now have a real answer instead of a vibe.

2 · Find your weak spots before somebody else does 🔍

Every organisation has holes. Mature ones know where theirs are. The gap between "we're secure" and "we know our top ten risks and who owns each one" is the gap between a company that gets surprised and one that doesn't.

The five ways to find weakness — and what each is actually for

MethodAnswers the questionWatch out for
Security audit"Are we doing what we said we'd do?"Compliance ≠ security. You can pass every audit and still get owned.
Vulnerability assessment"What known holes exist across our estate?"Broad but shallow. Produces thousands of findings and no priorities. The scan is the easy part.
Threat modelling"What could go wrong with this specific thing, by design?"Cheapest control in this table and the most skipped. Done on a whiteboard before code exists.
Penetration test"Can somebody actually chain these into real damage?"Narrow, expensive, point-in-time. A clean pentest means clean that week, in that scope.
Social engineering test"Will our people hand over the keys?"Run it to fix the process, never to punish the person who clicked. Punish clicks and you kill reporting — which is the control that actually matters.
Bo says: A vulnerability scanner will hand you 4,000 findings. That list is not a plan. The skill — the thing you get paid for — is turning 4,000 findings into "these six, this quarter, in this order, and here's why."

Risk, in plain terms

Risk isn't a feeling. It's roughly:

Risk = how likely is it × how bad would it be

Which means a critical-severity vulnerability on a lab box nobody can reach can matter less than a medium on the internet-facing system that holds customer records. Severity is about the flaw. Risk is about your business. Confusing the two is the most common mistake junior analysts make, and correcting it out loud is how you sound senior in a meeting.

Four honest options once you've named a risk:

  • Mitigate — reduce it. Patch, configure, segment, add a control.
  • Transfer — push the financial impact elsewhere. Insurance, contracts.
  • Avoid — stop doing the risky thing. Kill the feature, retire the system.
  • Accept — decide it's tolerable. Write it down and get a name on it. An accepted risk with an owner and a date is a decision. An accepted risk nobody signed is negligence with extra steps.

Attack surface: the number that should always be shrinking

Your attack surface is every place an attacker could touch you — public IPs, open ports, web apps, APIs, SaaS tenants, VPN endpoints, staff inboxes, third parties with a login. Most organisations cannot list theirs. That's not incompetence; things accumulate. A contractor spins up a demo box in 2021 and leaves.

You cannot defend what you don't know you own. Asset inventory is unglamorous and it is the foundation of everything else in this playbook. Every framework starts there for a reason.

Drill 2

Threat model something you use

Pick an app you use daily. Sketch it: users, front end, back end, database, third parties. Draw a box around what you control. Then, for each arrow crossing that boundary, ask the four questions: Can someone pretend to be someone else? Can they change something they shouldn't? Can they see something they shouldn't? Can they take the whole thing down?

Why: This is threat modelling. No tools, no budget, 40 minutes. It's also a portfolio piece — bring the diagram to an interview.

3 · Stay ahead — threat intel without the hype 📡

Threat intelligence has a marketing problem. Sold as a glowing map of the world with red arrows; usually delivered as a feed of IP addresses nobody looks at. Intel is only intelligence if it changes a decision. Otherwise it's a newsletter.

Three levels, and knowing the difference is itself a senior signal:

  • Strategic — for leadership. "Ransomware crews are increasingly targeting our sector through managed service providers." Changes budget and priorities.
  • Operational — for defenders. "This group is exploiting this product, this way, right now." Changes what you hunt for this week.
  • Tactical — for machines. Indicators: hashes, domains, IPs. Feeds the blocklist. Shortest shelf life — an attacker changes an IP in ten seconds.
The test for any intel feed: what will we do differently on Monday because of this? If the answer is "nothing," you're paying for anxiety.

Thinking like the attacker (the legal, useful version)

Attackers are not wizards. They're economically rational. They want the cheapest path to their goal. That's why phishing never dies — it's cheap and it works. Nobody burns a valuable zero-day on you when a 2019 CVE on your VPN is still open.

So when you're prioritising, stop asking "what's the scariest thing that could happen?" and start asking "what's the cheapest way in, from where they're standing?" Fix that. Then ask again.

Two boundaries, and they are not optional: authorisation and scope. Testing a system you don't own or haven't been contracted to test is a crime in most countries — the skill and the intent are irrelevant. Build the habit now, on your own lab, where the only thing at stake is your own machine.

Drill 3

Build a one-page intel brief

Pick your sector — healthcare, defense, finance, retail. Find three credible reports from the last year (CISA advisories, vendor threat reports, ISAC bulletins). Write one page: what's trending, why it matters to this sector, and three recommended actions. Executive tone, no jargon.

Why: This is a real deliverable in a real job — and one page beats a 40-slide deck every time.

4 · Measure your posture in numbers people believe 📊

"Security posture" means: given what you've got, how well would you actually hold up? It's three things, and the third is the one people underfund.

  1. Technology — what you've deployed, and more importantly whether it's configured and monitored. A tool nobody watches is a receipt, not a control.
  2. Process — what happens at 2am when the alert fires. Who's called, who decides, who talks to customers.
  3. People — whether staff report the weird email, and whether your team is trained and not burned out.

Metrics that survive contact with an executive

Bad metrics measure activity. Good metrics measure outcomes and risk.

Weak metricStrong metricWhy
"We blocked 4 million attacks"Mean time to detect (MTTD)Blocked traffic is weather. Detection time is capability.
"We ran 12 scans"% of critical vulns remediated inside SLAFinding them isn't the job. Closing them is.
"98% completed training"Phishing report rate (not just click rate)Completion measures attendance. Reporting measures behaviour.
"We have EDR"% of known assets with EDR installedCoverage gaps are where incidents live.
—Mean time to respond (MTTR)Detection without response is just knowing you're on fire.
Bo says: If your metric goes up when you do more work but doesn't move when you get safer, it's a vanity metric. Kill it. You'll spend less time in status meetings and more time in the actual job.

Maturity, honestly: most models run roughly ad hoc → repeatable → defined → managed → optimising. The useful move isn't scoring yourself a 5. It's being honest that you're a 2 in asset management and a 4 in incident response, and spending accordingly. Every organisation is uneven. Pretending otherwise is how the gaps stay hidden.

Drill 4

Score something you actually run

Take your own setup — home network, side project, or your team's environment with permission. Pick five NIST CSF outcomes (it's free to read) and score yourself 1–5 with one sentence of evidence each. Then write the single thing you'd fix first and why.

Why: Self-assessment against a public framework is exactly what a GRC or security-engineer interview asks you to demonstrate.

5 · Build the strategy — and get it funded 🧱

A strategy is not a tool list. It's a written answer to four questions: what are we protecting, from whom, to what standard, and what will we do first? If a document doesn't answer those, it's a wish list.

Start from a framework so you're not inventing gravity

  • NIST CSF — organises everything into Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Best shared language between security and leadership, because executives can follow it.
  • CIS Controls — a prioritised, opinionated list. Tells you what to do first. Best starting point when you have nothing.
  • ISO 27001 — a management system you can be certified against. Best when customers are asking for proof.
  • Zero trust — not a product, an operating principle: never trust by network location, verify every request, give the least access that works, assume breach.

Speaking business — the part that gets you promoted

Security doesn't make money, so it competes with things that do. Which means every proposal has to answer a question leadership is always silently asking: what does this let us do, or stop from happening?

Instead of: "We need $80K for a PAM solution."

Try: "Fourteen accounts can currently change production without review. Two are ex-contractors. One misuse is a multi-day outage and a customer-notification event. $80K removes that path and gets us the audit evidence the enterprise deal is asking for."

Same request. The second one gets funded, because it's denominated in their risk, not your tooling.

Bo says: The highest-paid people in security are the ones who translate. Tech → money, and money → tech. That's the whole job at the top, and you can start practising it at any level.
Drill 5

Write the one-page business case

Take the fix you identified in Drill 4. Write one page: the risk in business terms, what happens if it's ignored, the proposed fix, rough cost, and what changes once it's done. No acronyms without expansion. Give it to somebody non-technical and see if they can repeat it back.

Why: If they can repeat it, it'll get funded. If they can't, it won't — and that's true no matter how right you are.

6 · Use what you already own before you buy anything 🧰

Most organisations are running a fraction of what they've already paid for. Licences bought in a panic after an incident, deployed to 40% of the estate, in default configuration, with alerts going to a mailbox nobody opens.

Before any new purchase, four questions:

  1. Coverage — what percentage of known assets does the existing tool actually reach? (Then: how good is your asset list? Usually the real problem.)
  2. Configuration — is it in default/monitor-only mode? Default settings are built to avoid breaking things, not to stop attackers.
  3. Consumption — is anyone reading the output? An alert nobody triages isn't detection.
  4. Overlap — do three products already do this? Consolidating often buys more security than adding.
Bo says: "We already own it and it's turned off" is the most common finding in the industry. Walking in and fixing that on a budget of zero is how you make your name in the first 90 days.

When you do buy, judge on: does it close a risk we've actually named; can our team run it with the headcount we actually have; what does it integrate with; what does year three cost, not year one; and what's the exit if it disappoints. Run a proof-of-concept against your environment with your success criteria written down beforehand — every product demos beautifully on the vendor's laptop.

The whole playbook in nine lines

  • Get specific about who — the answer changes the defense.
  • Tactics outlive tools. Learn the sequence, not the product.
  • Severity is about the flaw; risk is about your business.
  • You can't defend what you don't know you own.
  • Intel that changes no decision isn't intel.
  • Measure outcomes, not activity.
  • A strategy answers what, from whom, to what standard, first.
  • Say it in money or it doesn't get funded.
  • Turn on what you already bought before buying more.
