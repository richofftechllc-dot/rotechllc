Module 1 · How AD Actually Works

ad1 · presenter: BO
What's good — Bo here. Before we break Active Directory, you gotta understand what it even is, because most people who say they "know AD" just know how to add a user. Here's the whole thing in one breath. Active Directory is the identity system for a company's network — it decides who you are, what you can touch, and which machines trust each other. The server that runs it is the Domain Controller. Own a Domain Controller, and you own the company. That's not dramatic, that's literal. On that controller is a file — NTDS dot dit. Every account, every password hash, in the whole domain. That's the crown jewel. An attacker reads that file, it's over. And the way you prove who you are is Kerberos — it's ticket-based. You log in once, you get a ticket, you reuse it. Hold onto that word, "ticket," because almost every attack we're about to cover is just a clever abuse of how those tickets get handed out. Last thing: service accounts. Accounts that run software instead of people. Over-privileged, no MFA, weak passwords that never change. That's the soft underbelly — remember it. That's the map. Take the module, and I'll see you in the next one.

Module 2 · Domain Recon & Attack Paths

ad2 · presenter: BO
Alright — you've got a foothold. One low-privilege user. Beginner move is to start swinging. Pro move is to stop and map. Here's why. Active Directory is generous — any authenticated user can read a ton about the domain. Users, groups, computers, service accounts, trusts. So before you touch anything, you draw the whole kingdom. But the real magic is this: modern AD attacks don't hunt vulnerabilities, they hunt paths. Picture it — your nobody user can reset a help-desk account's password. That help-desk account is local admin on a server. And a Domain Admin logged into that server this morning and left their ticket sitting in memory. That's three hops. No exploit anywhere. Just permissions nobody audited, chained together into game-over. Tools graph this for you — "who can reach Domain Admin, and how." And here's the part for the defenders watching: that's the same graph you run on your own domain. The attacker's map and your map are identical. See the path first, cut one link, close it. Enumerate hard. The finding is almost always sitting in plain sight. Take the module.

Module 3 · Credential & Kerberos Attacks

ad3 · presenter: BO → h/o to FLO
BO: This is the heart of it. And here's what makes these attacks nasty — they don't exploit bugs, they abuse features. AD working exactly as designed. Take Kerberoasting. Any user can request a service ticket — normal, expected, boring. But part of that ticket is encrypted with the service account's password hash. So you grab it, you walk away, and you crack that password offline on your own machine where nobody's watching. Weak service-account password that never expires? You just got real credentials and the domain never made a sound. Same energy with pass-the-hash — NTLM lets you log in with the hash itself, you never need the actual password. And pass-the-ticket — steal a Kerberos ticket straight out of memory and become whoever owned it. FLO: And this is where I come in, because every one of those has a clean defense. Kerberoasting dies the moment service accounts get long random passwords that rotate on their own — the offline crack just never finishes. Pass-the-hash shrinks when you reduce NTLM and lock down who has admin. The attack teaches you the fix — that's the whole point of learning it. Take the module, and know both sides.

Module 4 · Escalation & Domain Domination

ad4 · presenter: BO
Now the endgame — turning good credentials into total control of the domain. DCSync: you abuse replication to ask a Domain Controller for password hashes, as if you were another controller. With the right rights, you pull anybody's hash without ever logging into the DC. Golden ticket: this is the scary one. There's an account called KRBTGT whose key signs every single Kerberos ticket in the domain. Steal that key, and you can forge a ticket for anyone, with any privilege, and the whole domain trusts it. It's a skeleton key. And here's why AD breaches are so brutal to recover from — if an attacker got that KRBTGT key, resetting passwords does nothing. They can still forge tickets. To actually kick them out, you have to rotate that one master key, twice, with a specific procedure most companies have literally never run. But notice the pattern — almost all of this needs a privileged credential first. That's the defender's leverage, and that's the next module. Take this one.

Module 5 · Defending Active Directory

ad5 · presenter: FLO
Flo here — and this is the module that pays a blue-team salary, because now everything Bo showed you collapses into a priority list. If you do one thing to defend AD, do this: tiered administration. Keep Domain Admin credentials off ordinary workstations. Nearly every catastrophic AD breach ends the same way — a Domain Admin's ticket sitting on a machine the attacker reached. If that powerful ticket is never on the machine they land on, they can't steal it. That single control removes the most common road to total compromise. Then: least privilege and clean permissions, so there are fewer paths through the graph. Managed service accounts that rotate their own passwords, which kills Kerberoasting outright. Randomize the local admin password on every machine, so one crack doesn't unlock the whole fleet. And watch for the tells you now understand — replication coming from a machine that isn't a Domain Controller, that's DCSync. A sudden flood of service-ticket requests from one user, that's Kerberoasting. You prioritize better than someone who only read the hardening guide, because you've seen what the attacker reaches for first. That's the edge. Finish the track — you've earned it.
